# Intro

I want to tell you a little bit about Joseph M. Scriven. He was an Irish collegiate who graduated from Trinity college in 1842. After the death of fiance, Scriven would settle in Woodstock, Ontario among fellow Christians in the Plymouth Brethren. While there in 1855, he had heard of his mother’s sickness and crafted the words to a poem to encourage her. The poem was known as “Pray without Ceasing” and would later be renamed to “What a Friend We Have in Jesus.”[\*](/10165e4381b2809b8a24e3cef47a7e86#18f65e4381b2804ba8dcd2806ede0c2e) When posthumously published, this song was originally paired with [[Bible/ESV/1 Thessalonians/1 Thess 5#17|1 Thes 5:17]]

What a Friend we have in Jesus,  
All our sins and griefs to bear!
What a privilege to carry  
Everything to God in prayer!
O what peace we often forfeit,  
O what needless pain we bear,
All because we do not carry  
Everything to God in prayer!

I wanted to start with these lyrics tonight because this is a succinct summary of what James calls upon Christians to do in this passage. We should carry, “_everything_ to the Lord in prayer.”

Tonight we’ll see how James commands us to:

# **Pray for the suffering (v. 13)**

> [!question] ❓
> **What is the significance of the phrase “anyone among you?” Does that emphasize a person or people to pray for?**

Remember, James is writing to the diaspora.

> **
> Christians**
>
> “among” he is tying us with a crowd of people. Those that we are “among”, those that we are in the company of or members of.
>
> **Locally**
>
> “among you” we are sharing membership with them and they are with us. Most likely this means that James, in this context, is speaking about those around us. This would speak to the fact that those in the community know each other, they are well acquainted with one another. They know how to specifically pray for the one who is suffering.

Now is that to say that we should not pray for christians around the world and we should not pray for non-believers? No not at all, there is great evidence in Paul letters that he prayed for churches even though he was not present. I seek to emphasize what James is saying here so that we do not neglect this activity. How can we pray for those who suffer if we don’t know how they suffer? It requires us to be close with another and share how we suffer.

> [!question] ❓
> **What does James mean by suffering?
> **Doug Moo and Richard Plummer both define it as “any form of external or internal hardship.” This could be with an eye to some examples from last week:
>
> - Grumbling about another brother
> - Enduring hard oppression from a cruel boss
> - Fighting anger that wells up from within\*\*
>   \*\*

Put it in context, we are all awaiting the Lord’s return and will at one point or another endure suffering while we wait for His return. [[Bible/ESV/Hebrews/Heb 10#24|Hebrews 10:24-25]] would be later written, but that passage explicitly commands us to join a church community. The writer of Hebrews is smart and inspired by the Spirit! He sees the merit that James is saying here, those in a community carry upward the sufferings of our community in prayer.

# **Pray for the sunny (v. 13)**

> [!question] ❓
> **What does James mean by cheerful?
> **Doug Moo and Richard Plummer both define it as “internal disposition of happiness.” Signaling that this is not necessarily that their outward circumstances are happy, but that they are happy!

We are commanded yet again to pray but in a different way! James commands us to sing praise, or in some translations to sing psalms! This can certainly be achieved in prayer, but also in a very literal sense, in singing.

Have you thought about that before? That when you sing a song of worship that it is a prayer to God? Sometimes when we sing, it is a prayer for our heart to match the words we sing, “God please _make my heart_ like this.” In this situation, our heart _is matching_ the words we sing.

So when we sing a song like Rejoice by The Modern Post

> Rejoice, come and lift your hands and raise your voice
> He is worthy of all praise
> Rejoice
> Sing the mercies of your king

or We Give Thanks (Psalm 107) by Sovereign Grace Music

> We give thanks, O God
> for your steadfast love
> We give thanks, O God
> For You are good,
> Lord, you are good

In both these, we can sing them out with the fellow believers in thankfulness for the cheerful heart that he has put in us or in our brothers and sisters.

Again, this requires us to share with one another the things to be cheerful for, so that we can sing to the lord with joy and carry it with joyous prayer!

# **Pray for the sick (v. 14-16)**

v.14 - This guy is really sick!

- He has to call the elders to him
- Note that they pray “over” the one who is sick. Moo points out that this is most likely because the sick person is bed-ridden!
- “Anoint him” - physically with oil. Most likely as a religious way (not a medicinal way) of spiritual encouragement, a reminder that this brother or sister is set apart and holy. This same word is used in [[Bible/ESV/Mark/Mark 6#13|Mark 6:13]], where the disciples anoint those in who sick as they go out from Jesus to evangelize.

> [!question] ❓
> **Will this oil heal the sick brother or sister?
> **No, the prayer of faith will!

v.15 -

> [!question] ❓
> **James says that the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick in verse 15. What is “the prayer of faith?”**
> This is a prayer that recognizes the power of God and yet is open to God’s sovereign will.
>
> Plummer states that when we pray “in Jesus’ name” we are recognizing Jesus’ will into our prayer. Stating “in Jesus name” we are not simply invoking our advocate to carry our request to God the Father. Instead we are asking for Jesus to carry our request forth ONLY if the answer will bring him glory.

> [!question] ❓
> **Looking at that same verse (verse 15), what must the sick be saved from?**
> Most likely their physical illness.
>
> Yes, some commentators make a statement about spiritual salvation, but it does ignore the immediate context. This is about physical illness.
>
> “[It] is best understood as a typical promissory statement that does not necessarily list exceptions. We find many parallels in Jesus’ ministry (e.g., “Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you. For everyone who asks receives, and the one who seeks finds, and to the one who knocks it will be opened”; Matt. 7:7–8). Such invitations are made memorable by focusing attention on God’s gracious invitation and our rightful expectation of response.” - Plummer
>
> To summarize Plummer’s point. Essentially, you _can_ take this at face value. There will be exceptions, but a prayer delivered in faith will heal someone. Though exceptions do exist due to God’s sovereign will.

Why ask a question that is splitting hairs? Lately there’s been some discussion about interpretation on the boys side. We’ve spoken over how commentaries and seminary can feel necessary to be able to interpret a passage correctly. I want you to know that I spent time reading commentaries and they have gone into technical word study in the Greek, but I wanted you all to see that at the end of it all the plain interpretation is the main interpretation, this entire passage has to do with **physical illness**.

Man, I had to clear a lot of junk out of my heart and have a commentator share his perspective to see this clearly. I wanted to make excuses for God why some people are not healed and try to interpret this another way, but we can’t. It’s fact. God will heal those who are sick if we pray with faith. Faith in his power and faith in his will.

Not only that, but God will forgive those sins he has committed.

> [!question] ❓
> **Why mention the sick brother or sister’s sins?**
> A commonly held belief was that people were sick entirely because of their sin. We see this with the [[Bible/ESV/John/John 9#2|John 9:2]], “Rabbi who sinned this man or his parents that he was born blind?” So James is tackling this, but also we can take this plainly as well. Look to the next verse…

v.16 - Here we learn the Lord may restrict healing from us because we have not confessed our sin. This forms an interesting question

> [!question] ❓
> **Can sin cause sickness?
> **Absolutely, think of the drunkard who has liver damage, or the sexual immoral with STD. Sometimes the consequence of sin is sickness.

**Does that mean that sickness is always related to sin? **

> Certainly no! In John 9 Jesus answers the question in verse 3, [[Bible/ESV/John/John 9#3|John 9:3]]
>
> [3] Jesus answered, “It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him.

This to say, some sickness will be caused by sin. In the same way that we should be close enough to another to pray in the midst of suffering or in joy, so should we be close enough to confess the sin that has caused our suffering.

Doug Moo points out that it is not even the _elders_ that do the healing. It is the power of prayer that heals! See how the congregation is praying for this one who is sick and how they are healed. James affirms us that those who are righteous, wholly committed to God, have their prayers answered. So don’t be shy to confess, there are plenty of righteous people who take up your request in prayer!

# **Pray steadily (v. 17-18)**

James shifts into a small section to share how we should pray and gives us a practical example of a very righteous man. The prophet Elijah. It is not good for us to only carry this prayer for steadfastness in suffering, praise in cheerfulness, or healing for the sick just one time, but all time! Frequently!

Using the example of Elijah, he says that prays for 42 months, every single day, multiple times a day and it didn’t rain. It says that he prayed with fervor, intensity or passion. We might be inclined to think, that Elijah is something special and we could never do that, but look what James says, “a man with nature like ours!”

James is encouraging us to pray with similar fervor! Daily, in every moment. We need to carry all to to the Lord in prayer.

Not only that but may we look to grow in our righteousness and unlock similar power to Elijah’s righteous prayer. He exemplifies a strong prayer of faith, His will was so closely aligned to God’s will, that he prayed for what God desired, a drought. As a prophet, he knew this because he spoke with God. As regular Christians, I don’t think we’ll know if we need to pray for a drought, but we can certainly pray for the things in God’s revealed will, the Bible.

# **Pursue the stray (v. 19-20)**

James closes not only this section on prayer, but the entire letter with this small passage. While the action here is certainly pursuit, we can, and will, do so by physically meeting with this person and also praying to the Lord. In fact, while James doesn’t explicitly command it in this passage, I would certainly encourage it!

> [!question] ❓
> **What does it mean to “bring back a sinner from wandering”?
> **Evangelism! You have shared the gospel with this person and they believe in Christ.

[[Bible/ESV/1 John/1 John 2#19|1 John 2:19]] is clear that those that have left were not Christians to begin with,

“[[Bible/ESV/1 John/1 John 2#19|1 John 2:19]]

> [19] They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. But they went out, that it might become plain that they all are not of us.”
>
> With that in mind we know that this person has been saved.

> [!question] ❓
> **Who is the subject in verse 20? Why do you know this?**
> The saved person. As a Christian, your soul is already saved and covered from sin. This is more of a futher emphasis on how saved this person is.

> [!question] ❓
> \*\*I have to confess, I struggled to see how this passage tied in. Any thoughts from anyone?

”\*\*Inevitably, any true Christian reading James’s letter will experience conviction of sin and a desire for confession, repentance, and restoration. Readers will also likely be reminded of those formerly within the Christian community who have strayed from the fold. James ends his letter with a loving exhortation not to neglect the straying sinner but to seek him out with love and truth.” - Plummer

”He has spoken to his brother and sister about many problems: sinful speech, disobedience, unconcern about others, worldliness, quarreling and arrogance. Now he encourages every believer to take the initiative in bringing any who have ‘wandered from the truth in any of these way back into fellowship with God and community.” - Doug Moo

_James puts this in there for those who reflect on their sin and have been reminded of ways they may have pushed those out of or seen others pushed out of the community and out of faith._

# Outro

So we have talked about how we should carry all to the Lord in prayer. We should:

To circle back to what we discussed at the beginning, a curious fact about the poem “What a Friend We Have in Jesus” was that it was not published in any of Scriven’s works until much later after his death. Why? Scriven had said that the poem was for solitary reflection and encouragement as opposed to his other works which were for the congregation to sing. I think it is a joy that we can sing this song together, but perhaps reinterpret those lyrics as an exhortation and encouragement just as Scriven’s mother would have read it. Imagine you had recieved a letter with those lyrics.
