# Teaching Notes

## Intro

The year is 1839, the hot new play _Richelieu_ (Ree-sha-loo) has just premiered. The play’s surrounds the titular character, Cardinal Richelieu, a real-life 16th century cardinal who sought to consolidate the power of the French monarchy under King Louis XIII.

At the beginning of Act II we learn from Richelieu’s friend and confidant, Joseph, that there is a plot from the English to kill Richelieu. Richelieu, an elderly man now, hearing this from Joseph, humorously attempts to wield a large two handed sword as a display of strength holding it overhead before it immediately falls to the ground. Struck by his physical weakness he says, “_You see a child could slay Richelieu, now.”_

Francois, his page, encourages him by saying, _“But now, at your command are other weapons, my good Lord.” _

Richelieu, upon hearing this, seats himself at his nearby writing desk and utters words that have rung through history to the modern day,

The playwright, Edward Bulwer-Lytton, was the first to write those iconic words and had defined a core truth that we have seen in many times in history. **Words are often more powerful than physical weapons.** But did you catch the last part of that quote, it’s a significant part. “*Take away the sword, states can be saved without it!” *Edward, through the character of Richelieu, correctly claims that without physical might entire nations can be still be saved or ruined simply by the use of words. Words are powerful.

That is the focal point of **[[Bible/ESV/James/Jas 3#1|James 3:1-12]]**. The tongue, which is the source of the pen, is a powerful part of the body that must be used for good speech and not bad.

We’re going to take a look at:

## A Warning About Our Speech (v. 1-2)

James first starts by warning the entire church, not many should become teachers. (Note the “my brothers” part).

> [!question] ❓
> **Does James want no one to teach the bible?**
> No
> **Then why does he have this section here? What’s the significance?**
>
> Yes James is putting this hear as a warning about the seriousness of teaching. This judgement is meant to make teachers take the work seriously.
>
> Titus 2:1, “Teach what accords with sound doctrine”

Note the _we_ here. I enjoy that James, a biblical writer, ties himself in with fellow teachers. James reminds us that all (himself included) must take this role seriously.

Then he also points out how we all stumble. This is an explanation of why restraint should be exercised with becoming a teacher. James using his signature model, states, then expands. There are so many ways we all stumble in our sin, in our weakness.

> [!question] ❓
> **Why is it significant if a teacher “stumbles?”**
> Given his or her position (women can teach women). It can influence the brother or sister’s spiritual life negatively.
>
> [[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 18#6|Matthew 18:6]]
>
> [6] but whoever causes one of these little ones who believe in me to sin, it would be better for him to have a great millstone fastened around his neck and to be drowned in the depth of the sea.

James to conclude this warning focuses on one area in which we can stumble, our speech. “If anyone does not stumble in what he _says._” It makes sense for James to hone in on speech for the teacher. Teachers say a lot of words. The more words you say, the easier it is to stumble and say something regrettable. However, James has a word for those who can practice restraint. He says _“anyone who does not stumble in what he says, is a perfect man”_

Perfect. Commentators are a little split on this interpretation. Some claim “perfect” (**téleios** in Greek) means perfect. Meaning, only one man has done this perfectly, Jesus. Which means no one can fully do this other than Jesus.

Others argue the better interpretation here is “complete” or “mature” like how James uses “perfect” in 1:4, “perfect and complete.” It would mean a mature believer. I’d advocate for this understanding of “perfect” here.

And what happens to this mature believer, he is able bridle, to control, his whole body.

Said another way, **those who are mature in their speech are also able to be mature in their actions.**

So we see the warning here is to take seriously the responsibility of teaching, because it’s easy to stumble. Especially in how we talk! Keep in mind though, those who are mature in their speech are able to be mature in their actions.

It’s clear that discipline is required to keep from stumbling, but let’s take a look at *why *restraint is needed as we examine:

## The Power of Our Speech (v. 3-5a)

James compares the bit of a horse and the rudder of large ship to the tongue. He points to the power this small part has over the entire object.

> [!question] ❓
> **Why does James compare the tongue to a bit or rudder? What do we learn from that?**
>
> - Require focus and concentration to guide a horse or boat. If you loose focus, they will drift.
>   - Story about horseback riding Duke and his desire for snacks
> - Capable of harm to yourself if you “fall asleep at the wheel”
> - Capable of harm to others if you’re asleep at the wheel!

All in all, there is great _power_ in our speech. In verse 5, James says it “boasts” of great things. Commentator Robert Plummer points out that our inclination is to think of this boasting in the same way that the “little horn” atop the beast in Revelation “boasts.” Like a sinful, God forsaking boasting. However, this boasting is genuine. The power _is there_ for the tongue. It can boast of great things for it is able to do perform great acts of power.

> [!question] ❓
> **What are some good things we can do with our speech?**
>
> - Encourage
> - Teach
> - Worship
> - Express Thankfulness

Yes, we can do all of those things with our speech! Using the analogies James provides, that would be using our tongue to directing the horse to remain on the path. Or as a helmsmen, keeping the boat away from a reef or a sandbar. Both are able to transport and any cargo to our destination quickly!

Sadly the opposite is true too. If we lose focus, we let off the reigns or take our hands off the rudder, we’ll find that the large horse or ship, will start to chart its own course. Wandering into possible destruction and harm for you (the driver) and whatever you collide into. Which brings us to:

## The Destruction of Our Speech (v. 5b-9)

James continues describing the power of tongue, but how this power can get out of control! He compares it to a small spark that can set on fire an entire forest. He calls it a “world of unrighteousness.” Our speech can be a be sinful!

> [!question] ❓
> **What are some of the sins of our speech?**
>
> - Gossip
> - Slander
> - Cursing
> - Blasphemy
> - Lying

James explains that this fire can spread from the forest to our spiritual lives and set us aflame with hellfire. In contrast to the mature man who acts in a mature way, here is a man who has given in to sinful speech and has given up his pathway to Heaven. The consequences are very serious.

James wants us to know how easy it is for our speech to run wild and so he uses the analogy of taming animals. He points out that every animal has been tamed by man.

_Possibly_ some prophecy about Shamu here, no joking, but still, it’s quite incredible that James it right. I was listening to an old sermon Chris gave on this section and he pointed out that it is easy to train a 1,984 lbs Killer Whale to jump on to dry land and smile than it is to train the tongue.

In keeping with his animal analogy, James describes our speech as a restless evil. An evil that does not sleep, is always lurking ready to spring forth, and that it is full of poison. A long-lasting painful bite. Once we are bitten by another’s sinful speech, someone’s lies, gossip, hateful words. It hurts for a long-time, just like poison. I’m sure you have felt that before.

To accentuate the level of depravity our sinful speech can fall to, James points out the irony of how we can speak. First, we’ll praise and worship God. Then we’ll turn to someone who bears the image of our God. Someone who looks like God and was lovingly created by the same God that we love, and we curse them. We cut them down.

Rachelle has shared a story with me about a teacher who uses the microwave in the break room. She has a freezer meal, will stick it in the microwave for 6 minutes and sit down while it cooks. The other teachers will come in and loudly complain about this fact, “Oh my gosh! 6 minutes! That’s way too long for the microwave!” “Oh wow, it smells terrible too!” The meal this teacher has cooked “bears her image” in that it is the meal she has chosen and paid for and is probably looking forward to eating. Yet, her coworkers are berating her treasured meal. Have they said anything bad about her? No not a thing! However, by insulting her food, they have insulted her. It’s the same thing with how we curse other people. \*\*When we curse people, we curse God.

Robert Plummer writes, \*\*“Even a wicked person, who has perhaps cursed or harmed us, is still a broken portrait of God’s image.”

So what are we to do?

> [!question] ❓
> **James says no one can tame the tongue, so what’s the point? Is James being defeatist?**
> v. 10, “these things out not to be so.” Seems to indicate that it is undesirable for this way to continue.
>
> v. 10-12 point to the need to wrangle our speech and to be consistent with our Christ-like character

## The Fruit and Root of Our Speech (v. 10-12)

James uses three illustrations to close this section on the tongue. A spring, plants, and pond. James asks if it possible for a sprint to have fresh and salt water pour forth. The answer is an obvious no. Certainly, even if the spring was “brackish” a little bit of salt water would ruin the fresh water.

It recalls to mind [[Bible/ESV/1 Corinthians/1 Cor 5#6|1 Cor 5:6]] where Paul, talking about a sin of speech, says,

> _[[Bible/ESV/1 Corinthians/1 Cor 5#6|1 Corinthians 5:6]]
> [6] Your boasting is not good. Do you not know that a little leaven leavens the whole lump?_

Even a small bit of yeast makes a bread rise. So does even a bit of salt water make the spring no longer fresh.

To make the point more James describes how strange it would be for a fig tree to bear olives, or a grapevine to have figs. Springs always have one type of water come from it. Trees have one can of fruit grow on them.

Finally a salt pond will never yield fresh water.
