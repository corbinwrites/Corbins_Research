## Problem with Passion (v. 1-3)

James starts with a simply question, “Why are you fighting?” Clearly, we gain some helpful historical insight, the Jewish diaspora are fighting a lot! Worse still they are fighting within the church and with each other.

Thankfully, he quickly gives us the root. Though it is a surprising one. It is our passions _within_ us. How fascinating that the *external *conflict we face is the result of an _internal_ conflict within us. This war of passions.

> [!question] ❓
> **What is a “passion?”**
> Something we love. Something we cherish and desire. Something brings pleasure or is a desire for pleasure.

> [!question] ❓
> **What are the passions at war within us?**
> Most likely our desire for God and for the world. Verse 4 would point to that dichotomy.
>
> The thought is further developed by the nature of the word war. It necessitates two sides (at least) that are in opposition to one another. There would need to be a point of disagreement between the passions.

“Murder” - James could have the words of Jesus in mind (Matthew 5)

_“You have heard that it was said to those of old, ‘You shall not murder; and whoever murders will be liable to judgment.’\***\*But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment; whoever insults his brother will be liable to the council; and whoever says, ‘You fool!’ will be liable to the hell of fire.\*\***”_

With this interpretation, we would conclude that believers were most likely not killing each other to gain possession of something desired, but rather were “murdering in their hearts.”

However, I’d like to suggest Douglas Moo’s interpretation here as I think it is most clear in the text

Here James suggests that that murder _is on the table_. This is a possibility when our worldly passions are winning in our life.

Note that this this is NOT coveting, that is below. We _desire_ something so intensely that we are emotionally, perhaps even physically, killing people around us. From a “murder in our heart perspective” we have may have lived it out or seen others around us live it out. Some examples might include:

- Desire for a relationship propels you into a sinful relationship. Your desire for this person is so great you “kill” the friendships and Godly council around you.
- Desire for comfort propels you into an isolated lifestyle. Your desire for comfort and having things your way has led you to “kill” your time with people, seeking only to do things that meet your needs by yourself.
- Desire for a certain job propels you into sinful pursuit. You “kill” those around you in a slanderous way so as to elevate yourself as the best candidate for the job.

Going back a sentence, we see clearly that this desire that leads to murder, is within us and is **not** a Godly passion. Here we have chosen a side on the war of passions, and sadly it is the side that will lose when Christ comes to judge.

“Covet” - this is the desire for something someone has. This propels us to “fight and quarrel.” Essentially, arguing or hostile bargaining over something.

Note the vague subject here. Because we cannot obtain what, or whom do we fight quarrel about? We can fight and quarrel about two things, we can fight and quarrel with the owner of the object, or about the object being coveted (not necessarily with the owner).

Two examples:

- There is someone in the church who serves how I want to serve. I argue with them that they should let me do that role and take their job. Here I argue with the owner, because they have the object I desire.
- I am single and desire a relationship, however I covet this to a degree that has placed it above other Godly things. I have wise counsel in my life call out this sin. Rather than submit, I argue and perhaps even twist scripture to suit my purpose, “This is the most important thing in my life! Even Elliot said it was ok three Sundays ago. My primary goal in life is to be fruitful and multiply!”

Going back two sentences, we see clearly that this coveting that leads to arguing, is within us and is **not** a Godly passion. Again, we have chosen a side on the war of passions, and sadly it is the side that will lose when Christ comes to judge.

James concludes this verse with a bit of irony. We could modernize James words to say, “If you want it so bad, just ask God.”

It’s incredible how easy James makes this. Here we have two examples of a believer fighting tooth and nail to keep or obtain their desire and doing so through sinful means. James, almost shrugging while writing this says, “God has not given you your desire, God has not allowed you to keep this object, simply because you have not asked.”

James, quickly, qualifies this claim. He almost anticipates the listeners to say to him, “Well James! I _have_ asked and God hasn’t given it to me. So what about that?”

James explains, ,”We do not receive because we ask wrongly.” We desire to spend it on our passions.

## Prescriptions for Peace (v. 4-10)

### **Choosing friendship with God is our first step to peace with others**

James is outraged! You adulterers!

Why the sudden strong language?

See that when we are asking for something to spend on our earthly gift, rather than asking for a God glorifying gift we are committing spiritual adultery. Said another way, rather than show appreciation to bridegroom who loves us dearly and has granted us a beautiful ring, we instead pawn off that ring to do what we want!

James emphasizes through repetition that this decision puts us as enemies with God. (verse 4 and verse 5). This friendship with our worldly passions has put us on the wrong side of the passion war.

An appropriate illustration for this would be the idea of “No Man’s Land” that sprung from the trench warfare in World War I. In this war, both countries would dig trenches for miles. Trenches gave soldiers safety to avoid bullets and, depending on the distance between the front lines of trenches, even safety from hand grenades. The space between the trenches was referred to as “No Man’s Land” because, no man would want to go there or was presently there. Each side carefully watched the other trench so if anyone jumped over the trench wall and attempted to get to the other side they would be quickly gunned down.

When we attempt to live with both our passions for non-God glorifying things and God glorifying things, we find ourselves squarely in the middle of No Man’s Land and we will be gunned down as an enemy of God and a friend of the world.

So the temptation maybe for us to find “safety” in the trenches of “the world” however, [[Bible/ESV/Mark/Mark 16#33|Mark 16:33]] talks about how Jesus has already overcome the world and Revelation shares the picture of how Jesus will come to lay waste the earthly trenches. Sadly there is no safety even in the earthly trenches! We should instead choose friendship with God.

**Choosing friendship with God is our first step to peace with others.**

## Practical Peace (11-13)

James’ detail here points to a few things:

7. The Church is really struggling with their tongue/speech at this point.
8. James wants the seriousness of this one particular to be driven home to his readers. He does not want to leave any open room for bad interpretation. That is not to say the previous commands are any less important, in fact, in application you mind find one of them _is_ more important to you.
9. How important it is for us to watch our speech!

James gives the final command for this section in simple terms in verse 11,

“Do not speak evil against one another.”

> [!question] ❓
> **What does it mean to speak evil against one another? Any examples?**

The LSB renders this as “slander.” Which is a good, but more narrow translation. Slander describes “giving someone a bad name.” This could be through gossip or complaint and typically implies that person is not in the room with you.

However, “speak evil” is more broad. The person could be in the room with you and you could be berating to their face. Speaking evil, speaks to the *intent *of the speech. You intend evil, no matter if they present or not.

**Remember we’re talking about peace. If you want to keep peace, don’t speak evil!**

James explains that those that speak against a brother also speaks agains the law. The phrase “speaks against” is the idea that you are opposed to the subject. You are on the opposite side of the subject. You are opposed to them. That object is an enemy. How sad that when we speak against a brother that we find ourselves as their enemy AND the enemy of the Bible. Here we are back to being enemies of God.

> [!question] ❓
> \*\*But let’s talk about that word, judges. What do you think James means by judges?

\*\*“Judges” here most likely means “condemns”

An important distinction! As we talked about, it will be important for us as believers to practice “judgement” often. Perhaps a better word is “discernment.” We weigh what is good and bad in ourselves, in situations around us, and the people around us. That is to say though that while we make “judgement calls” many many times, we are not judges.

A judge has one privilege that we are not afforded as humans. Condemnation. Rendering punishment for crimes/sin.

It makes sense that James includes this. Isn’t this our natural reaction when we are in argument with someone? To condemn them? That person is wrong and their ways are wrong and evil. Haven’t we heard in arguments on the street that one person wants another to, “Go to Hell?”

> [!question] ❓
> **James says when we condemn those around us we also condemn the law? Why?**
> Because the law is a law of liberty (James 1:25, 2:8). By saying, “You’re not good enough.” We are equally saying, “The law is not good enough.” This person should be condemned not given new life. A weighty thing to render condemnation and to be “above the law.”

James says if we simply go around condemning people we are not actually being a doer of the law, but a judge. By putting the category of judge at odds with the “doer” of the Law, James is reminding us of what he has previously written! Anything that is NOT a doer is **worthless**. Including being a “judge” of the law and other brothers.

James points out that there is only one judge who can fully condemn and that is Jesus. We ain’t Jesus. At no point in our life can we look to our brother and sister in Christ and say, “that’s it, you’re out of Heaven!” That’s not our call. Nor can we say to anyone on the street, “I revoke your access to Heaven.” Again, not our job.

Ultimately it is the Lord who knows the person’s fate. Our job is to guide those to the best of our ability and to share our concerns with that brother and sister when we see them start to stray. Our job is _not_ to say who is going to heaven and who is going to hell.

Looking back at the beginning of the chapter James talks about how we are able to have peace with one another. It starts within ourself. We must take the winning side of the war of passions and choose a passionate devotion to God. To do so, James has given us many commands and promises to help with this endeavor:

- Submit yourselves to God
- Humble yourselves to God
- Resist the Devil
- Draw near to God
- Cleanse your hands
- Purify your hearts
- Be wretched and mourn and weep
- Do not speak evil against one another
- Do not condemn
