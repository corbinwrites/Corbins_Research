# Intro

The year is 335 BC, a class of students are eagerly waiting around for the their teacher to arrive. The teacher has been putting together groundbreaking information on the nature of communication and language, he calls it Rhetoric. This study combined with logic and dialectics would go on to make the study of philosophy, but that doesn’t concern us tonight. Rhetoric is a tool for practical debate, persuading a general audience using probably knowledge to resolve practical issues, his lesson for the day would be on _Antithesis_.

Antithesis, from an Aristotelean mindset is the idea of strengthening an argument by using exact opposites or simply contrasting ideas. They make a sentence or concept more memorable through the balance. An example from [[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 10#34|Matthew 10:34]], “I came not to bring **peace** but a ** sword**.” We see this idea of peace being contrasted with the instrument of war. It makes the line memorable since it has the large contrasts.

> **Where do you see antithesis in this passage? What concepts or themes are contrasted from one another?**
>
> - rich and poor
> - non-christian and christian
> - punishment and reward
> - indulgence and patience
> - fraud and honesty

Tonight, we will find that James practices _antithesis_ to highlight the**Pearl that is Patience**. The true prize within this passage, and an area of application we will focus on tonight, is for us to take away is verse 7, we were are commanded to be patient until the coming of the Lord. A good jeweler sets a gem upon a bed of black velvet to make it shine, in a similar way James sets the pearl of patience on a black velvet bed of depravity. We’ll look at this passage in two major sections:

1. Wrath for the Rich 5:1-6
2. Prize of Patience 5:7-12

# Wrath for the Rich (Non-Believers) - 5:1-6

As we start James sets the scene with a look at utter depravity, this is the black velvet for our pearl tonight. The dark part that makes the latter part, shine.

Note that unlike verse 7, the phrase “brothers” is not used here indicating they are non-believers. For these non-believers, we can see that there is **decisive, guaranteed language** of “miseries” coming for the rich here.

## Fraud (v. 4, held back wages)

Define: Wrongful or criminal deception intended to result in financial or personal gain; Intended to deceive others

v. 4 - Here the _wages_ of the laborers cry out against the rich. That’s pretty bad when the cash in your bank account starts condemning you against the Lord.

Do notice that the wages were kept in fraud. This means that these non-believing rich promised this to the laborers and it was not given to them. Imagine how frustrating that would be to have labored in a field for 8-10 hours a day and to not receive a single coin for your work. It is understandable that the harvesters cry out at this injustice! They could have worked the field of a noble landlord and received pay.

## Oppression (v. 4, 6)

Define: prolonged cruel or unjust treatment or control.

verse 4 also contains evidence of oppression. While the rich defrauding the harvester of money is grounds to cry, it’s possible that it could have been worse. The rich could have physically abused or injured the harvesters as well. If we look down to verse 6, we see that is well within the realm of possibility as the rich “have condemned and murdered the righteous.” This could be the harvesters or others. Not especially how the one who is oppressed is not resisting. This speaks to how the righteous person is innocent and living in a Christ-honoring manner. It also speaks to the the evil of this rich.

## Self-Indulgence (v. 3, 5)

Define: Unrestrained, Casual, without check or limitation, characterized by doing or tending to do exactly what one wants, especially when this involves pleasure or idleness. -Oxford dictionary

This same phrase is used in [[Bible/ESV/1 Timothy/1 Tim 5#4|1 Tim 5:4-5]] and explicitly condemned. In fact it calls those living in this lifestyle as _dead_ even while they live.

To take this a step further, these rich are “laying up treasure despite it being the last days.” That phrase is used frequently to refer to Jesus’ eminent return.

> [!note]+ Silly illustration

At the end of the week, you will need to give up all the equipment that belongs to the company. You gather all the desks in your area into one mega desk. You take the fake plants together and build a small forest. Anything that you want to do in the office you do.

The company owns all the staplers, desks, plants and they’ll be reclaimed at the end of the week, but you are “rich” for the time being. Rather than doing the work for the life after the office closes, you have simply gathered things that make you happy.

James has a dire illustration of this point. He calls this self-indulgent mindset as “fattening your heart.”

## Responses

### Repent, Non-believer

If this is you, repent! If you always give way to your earthly desires. If you seek to harm others for your own gain. If you are continually seeking to lie for your own gain. It’s quite possible you may not be saved right now today. We know these qualities are sinful: fraud, oppression, and self-indulgence are condemned in [[Bible/ESV/Ephesians/Eph 5|Ephesians 5]], Galatians 5, [[Bible/ESV/Colossians/Col 3|Colossians 3]]. So turn away from it!

### Don’t even be this a little, Christian

I do want to emphasize that this section is written to non-believers, but let us also examine our hearts as Christians and ensure we’re not giving a foothold for this sinful self-indulgent desire. Do you find yourself giving yourself to every desire you have? Being angry whenever you desire? Instantly gratifying whatever you want the second you want it? Do you find yourself storing experiences, money, pleasure, in excess in some way? While this is written to non-believers, it’s critical we examine where we could be tempted in such a manner and to give it up.

### Reflect on if you have felt this

Maybe you have felt the effects of the non-believing rich. You have been defrauded of money owed to you. You have been oppressed or abused in some way. You have seen the excess luxury of the rich and cried out about the injustice of it. In some way, you might see yourself as the antithesis to these non-believing rich, well my Christian brother let’s read on and see our response.

# Commands to the Christians - 5:7-12

In verse 7, James uses the word “therefore”, meaning he is pointing back to what he just wrote in verses 1-6. He is contrasting the rich non-believers with the poor Christians. Remember the recipients of the letter were mostly poor. This is due to the fact that they were persecuted in Roman, Greek, and Jewish society. _Some_ were rich, but they have the same instructions here and were rightly, not acting like the unbelieving rich.

With this eye to the terrible conditions the rich have put them under, James seeks to give confidence the Christians with two commands that are antithetical to how the rich act.

## Patience

In great contrast to the self-indulgent, luxury filled life of the rich who take what they want when they want it, James commands patience. James tells them to wait.

We see this patience manifest in 4 ways: Patiently wait for the lord, be patient with one another, be patient in trial, and remain patiently honest

He tells them to specifically wait for the coming of the Lord.

> [!question] ❓
> **What is the significance of the Lord’s return, in the context of this passage? Why would this matter to a Christian in these circumstances?**
>
> - Justice
> - Being removed from the situation

James likens it to a precious fruit. Just think about the best fruit you’ve ever eaten. Maybe a cold, crispy grape or a large slice of watermelon on a hot summer day. For that to get there, it had to endure many rains storms and take time to grow. James uses this illustration to point out that is the case for us as well. We must patiently endure difficult storms and wait a length of time and we will be rewarded with the sweet fruit of Christ’s return. When everything is made right again!

but this patience is more active than simply being a grape on a vine. We are commanded to establish our heart for the Lord is coming! Note the contrast from a fattened heart, this is instead an “established” heart.

> [!question] ❓
> **What does he mean to establish our hearts?**
> Another word used is “strengthen” our hearts in the LSB.
>
> Stabilize, shore up, fortify. These are images used to strengthen a structure. Just like how people put plywood on their house before a hurricane comes through. They fortify the windows to endure hard winds, stack sandbags to route water away and around the house. It looks for cracks in the foundations and repairs.
>
> This is a heart that has fortifications in place. It is a heart that has repaired weaknesses. It is a heart ready to endure.

> [!question] ❓
> **What does he mean by our “hearts” in establish our hearts?**
>
> - Innermost being
> - Our internal thoughts, emotions, actions the thing that motivates our outward behaviors

> “The Bible uses “heart” to describe the inner person. Scripture divides the human being into two parts, the inner and outer being. The outer person is your physical self; the inner person is your spiritual self (Eph 3:16). The synonym the Bible most often uses for the inner being is the heart. **It encompasses all the other terms and functions used to describe the inner person (spirit, soul, mind, emotions, will, etc.)** These other terms do not describe something different from the heart. Rather they are aspects of it, parts or functions of the inner person.**The “heart” is the real you.** It is the essential core of who you are. Though we put a tremendous amount of effort on the outer person, we all recognize that the true person is the person within.” - P.59 _Instruments in the Redeemer’s Hands by Paul David Tripp_

Using Paul David Tripp’s helpful definition we can think of establish our heart as strengthening, fortifying, our spirit, soul, mind and emotions. We do this because we have some storms to endure. We do this because we need to wait for our Lord to return. He will bring true justice.

> “By “heart” James refers to the innermost part of the person, which will be tempted to wither in resignation or strike out in retribution. Instead, the internal compass of a Christian should be set on the “due north” of the Lord’s return. We can be strong internally, not because we have the means to effect our own justice but because of the imminent “coming” of the all-powerful Judge who is also our Lord (v. 8). **A child abused by a bully on the playground will stand taller when he sees the school’s principal approaching.** ” -Plummer

### Patience with one another!

This patience that requires us to establish our hearts requires something else of us to as well, to keep peace.

James writes that we are not to grumble against one another. In this verse he’s specifically calling out, grumbling against fellow believers.

> [!question] ❓
> **What does James mean by do not grumble against one another?**
> This is also the word groan, mumble, murmur. The idea that you have something critical to say against your brother, but maybe you’re not going to make waves.

Consider the context of this command. The Christians have hard oppression on them from all sides. The last thing you would want is a fellow brother/sister also making your life hard. Consider the context in our lives. We all live through tough stuff each and every week, would you want someone grumbling against you in the midst of that? No way. Don’t do that to others.

James points out that those who are grumbling are liable for judgement. Worse than that, the judge is watching you. He is standing in the door watching you actively grumble. Imagine that. Here we’re being told to not grumble. It is like a parent telling a child, don’t play with a ball in the house and to watch the child get up, pick up a ball, and promptly throw it right at a lamp. It would be ridiculous!
So James in a similar way says, hey Jesus is watching. Don’t grumble!

James, in his signature way, pivots and expands to being

### **Patient in trial** First we are to be patient with each other, fellow believers inside the church, but we are also to be patient with *external *events, trials, outside of the church.

> [!question] ❓
> **In verse 10, James, speaking about a good example of suffering and patience, recalls prophets who spoke the name of the Lord. What are some examples you have? How do they show that?**

In a recall all the way back to Chapter 1, James reminds us that those who are patient are steadfast and that those who are steadfast are blessed.

As proof of this James invokes the story of Job. James affirms that Job, who is afflicted heavily by Satan _does_ remain steadfast. So next time someone tells you otherwise, point them to this passage. Job’s steadfastness isn’t perfect, but it *is *faithful, let that be of encouragement to you.

> A reader of the book of Job may rightly ask, “Did Job really remain steadfast? Didn’t he complain a lot?” Yet, even while fully expressing his lament to God, Job did not take his wife’s advice to “curse God and die” (Job 2:9). In the end, even though Job was humbled and faced correction (38:1–3; 40:1–5; 42:1–6), he was commended by the Lord both at the beginning (1:8) and at the end (42:7–8) of the book. -Robert Plummer

James says that the purpose of the Lord for Job was ultimately to bless him. We see how Job is blessed with wealth and a new family. James is not promising us the same thing here. Instead he is pointing to the qualities the Lord shows there, a heart of compassion and a desire to generously give. Even if you are not necessarily given a new family and wealth at the end of your affliction, we know we will have great riches in Heaven by being with our brother Jesus.

God is compassionate and merciful. He seeks to love you. He will give mercy to you for your transgressions. James tells us God is here to share that love and mercy with you, but wait for him. Be steadfast. Endure the trials, his purpose is to share compassion and mercy with you so that he recieves glory!

### Patiently remain honest

James finishes with a final command that**runs antithetical to the rich**. Instead of fraud, James encourages us to be honest.

> [!question] ❓
> **How do you interpret the command in verse 12?**
> Instead of saying an answer with the intent of pulling something back, just say what the honest answer is. If you commit to something, see it through. If you can’t make it, explain why.

Plummer’s thoughts:

> _How does this relate to James’s letter? When suffering injustice, one can be tempted to protect oneself through deceptive speech. Perhaps James here mentions oaths to call suffering Christians to both endurance of heart and integrity of speech when the pressures of life would lead many to despair or compromise. - Robert Plummer_

Our inclination in our patience could be to try to take matters into our own hands. I could just lie about this and it would go better! No says James! Remain patient. The Lord is coming. Continue to exercise honesty. Implicit in this command of course is that this will also point back to unity. I’d be pretty frustrated with someone who commits to doing something then doesn’t do it, so James again has a mind to those sins of the tongue once again.

# Outro

So as we have examined in the passage tonight, we have seen the intense wickedness of the rich and the wrath that will pour out on them contrasted with the meek patience of the Christians. By presenting this antithetically, James has magnified great the patience must be. It must be able to endure the fraud-filled, self-indulgent, oppressive schemes of the rich. The patience must endure the daily discouragement of our lives, the grumblings of others, and even prophetic level trial.
