# Teaching Notes

## Intro

I want to share a quick, frequently experienced story while I was at Hillside. Imagine, a young, unbearded Corbin. Bright-eyed, full of life, and looking to hang out with anyone and everyone. Many times, I’d meet someone new and exchange phone numbers with them. Sometimes it was busy or I’d have somewhere to be, but I would assure my new friend that we would coordinate later on a time and place to meet.

Some hours, maybe some days pass and I’d send a text. This new friend would unlock their phone and…

_Show of hands who are my iPhone users here?_

Yes. This person would see it staring back from their screen. The dreaded _green_ bubble of an android user.

One friend of mine, shared with me how much of a hassle my android phone was. He maintained two group chats, one with all the iPhone users of the ministry we served in and one with me in it. I was actually left out of things because he would forget to put things in the cross-platform group chat.

I had fallen prey to the sin of partiality. In a minor, silly way, I was treated differently due to an external factor, my trusty Motorola Moto G. How easy it can be for us fall prey to this sin.

> [!question] ❓
> **Can anyone give an example of some other ways that we can show partiality to someone?**
>
> - Wealth
> - Fashion/How they are dressed
> - Social Status
> - Car
> - Technology
> - Academics
> - Profession/Trade
> - Employer
> - Ethnicity
> - Color of Someone’s Hair

A consistent theme in partiality is that it is **external**, in some way. Specifically it is defined as “making judgements and distinctions based on external factors” according to Doug Moo. This man drives a porsche so he is rich. This man wears pants with holes in them so he is poor… or a worship leader.

It’s important for us to state that partiality is showing favor to one at the cost of another. It creates a false dichotomy. I must choose this person OR this person. Impartiality is the understanding that you can show favor to _both_.

## Case Study of Partiality (v. 2-7)

Before we dive in, I want to point out that this is an example. This example is true and most likely this pointed to true circumstances that were occurring in the diaspora at the time. Keep in mind, this is not the only form of partiality that James is talking about. He says for us to show no partiality in any circumstances, like the examples you gave earlier.

### Lenses (v. 2-4)

I want to use some “lenses” to examine verses 2-4. I use the phrase lens as analogy for how to examine this passage. Much in the same way that some one would wear a blue-tinted sunglass the lens blocks other colors and you see blue more richly. So can we put on one of these lenses to see something more clearly in this passage.

**Partiality in Hospitality**

Notice that the poor and rich person are described as “man”, not as a brother. This points to them joining the congregation of believers and being an “outsider.” This is reinforced by what is written about the rich being the ones who oppress and blaspheme (not typically Christian characteristics). We could think of this as someone visiting City Light for the first time. This partiality of wealth is expressed by helping the rich person to his seat, but the poor man is forced to sit on the floor. James writes that we are a judge with evil thoughts. We are making an internal judgement to show preferential treatment to one and not another. This forces us to think about partiality with who we choose to show hospitality to. **We should show it to \*\*\***all.\*_\*\* _ The man who is a bit dirty, as well as the woman who is put together.

**Partiality in conflict (less probable but worth mentioning)**

Instead of examining through the use of man, some commentators examine through the word judge and point out the use of the word “assembly” *(synagogue *in Greek) could mean this is a different type of meeting: A meeting to settle a dispute! The early church would call church-wide meetings to settle matters between members. These men walking in could be in dispute between each other and here before a word is spoken or an argument heard, preference is shown to the rich man, not to the poor man. I personally feel this application is a bit less defensible, however, I bring it to point out that when James writes “show no partiality” he does not only mean for all external factors, but also in **all situations**! In meeting new people or when witnessing conflict, we choose a side based on externals and we should not!

**Partiality in pride**

Look at the character of “you” in this verse (you pay attention to the one who wears fine clothing, and you say to the poor man). Do you see the pride in this character. Here he sees himself as above the poor man, not offering his chair. Instead he demands he sit on the floor or against the back wall. This man thinks only of himself!

That selfish thinking is most likely present with the rich man too.

> [!question] ❓
> **Why would someone be willing to give up their good seat to a rich man, if they have selfish intent?**
> They might want the rich man to do something for them! They want to have his good favor!

This is the crux of James case study. In the same way that we have earlier talked about how temptation is enticed by desire, James shows how that desire is contrary and hypocritical to God’s will.

### Hypocrisy (v. 5-7)

You can hear the desperate plea, “Listen”

Don’t we know that God has chosen the poor to be rich in the heaven of God? James here of course has in mind the beatitudes from Jesus:

> _[[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 5#3|Matthew 5:3]]
> [3] “Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven._

As well as Jesus’ lesson to the Rich Young Man:

> _[[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 19#23|Matthew 19:23-24]]
> [23] And Jesus said to his disciples, “Truly, I say to you, only with difficulty will a rich person enter the kingdom of heaven. [24] Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for a rich person to enter the kingdom of God.”_

We all know it is not impossible for the rich to enter Heaven (even James concedes this point with the phrase, _“He has promised to those who love him”_). However it is far easier for the poor to enter Heaven.

> [!question] ❓
> **Why is it easier for the poor to enter heaven? Why would God choose those who are poor in this world to be rich in faith?**
>
> - They are humble they cannot do anything without help. That’s what God is looking for.
> - They are satisfied with very little blessing and appreciate whatever is given. They are thankful. That is what God is looking for.

James can leave the point there, but turns up the heat to point out that the rich do not have passive indifference to these believers, but they are in outright resistance to them. They oppress them. They bring them into court to pay fines that might be impossible to pay. Worst still they speak against our God.

This is the person that we are showing partiality to. They may be dressed nice, but they are wicked underneath. Why would we give up kindness for another to show kindness to a wicked man? Could we not show kindness to both?

## Crime of Partiality (v. 8-11)

We are called to “love our neighbor” here he quotes Leviticus 19 and Jesus in [[Bible/ESV/Mark/Mark 12#31|Mark 12:31]]. A high calling. In the same way that we look out for our own needs so should we look to do for others.

Those that do not do this and show partiality are committing sin and are “convicted by the law as transgressors.” They are committing a crime against the Law. Which we know the Law to be, the Bible.

> [!question] ❓
> **Read verses 10-11. What does James mean here?**
> We are called to keep the law. If you ignore or break one part of it you’re in trouble for all of it.
>
> Analogy: Think of how many factors there are in driving a car. You could be the most careful driver. Stopping fully at every stop sign. Observing the speed limit. Signaling with every turn. You’ve followed all the rules, except… You have no brake lights. Well sadly, you’re going to be pulled over and given a ticket. That’s great that you’re safe driver, but you’ve still broken road safety laws and are a transgressor of the law. You’re guilty of violating the whole of “traffic laws.”

Unlike a broken tail-light though, James uses the example of adultery and murder. Two sins I think we would consider “big.” He uses it to illustrate the point that the one who commits adultery is just as guilty as the one who commits murder. However, notice that little word at the beginning of verses 10, and 11. “For.” Incredibly, James has connected partiality to both adultery and murder. Those who are guilty of partiality are just as great of a law breaker as those who cheat on their spouse or take another’s life.

## Care of Impartiality (v. 12-13)

James issues another two commands

> [!question] ❓
> **What does James command?**
> To **speak** as those who are judged under the law
>
> To **act** as those who are judged under the law

I love that he uses these two words, not just in our speech but in the work of our hands, feet, and minds. Every facility in our body is to be used to accomplish the task of following the law.

> [!question] ❓
> **Define the law of liberty. Your definition should reflect both what the law and liberty mean. How does one “speak and act”” while under the law of liberty?**
> We are to follow this law but are free from penalty due to Christ’s sacrifice (see verse 13)

James reminds us how judgement is rendered. It is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy.

> [!question] ❓
> **What judgement is James talking about here?
> **Final Judgement - [[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 25#31|Matthew 25:31-46]] “Depart from me you cursed”

Yes, James explains that mercy will be withheld, not given, to those who do not show mercy. This thought links back to our idea of partiality and makes an interesting point.

Impartiality, not being partial, is a mercy. It’s care for another person.

This work is a mercy, “Compassion or forgiveness shown toward someone whom it is within one's power to [punish](https://www.google.com/search?sca_esv=ca2c68be96a7cdb5&sxsrf=ADLYWILOoONRQq2s1ytsvK64_cEhcKeOtA%3A1730770330991&q=punish&si=ACC90nwdkA2npcVVmNPViiSe8FMKZ-0wjr10ILtkUqzddXzf_n5cD6RYs6GKmzHAUvJSKNaScSEjJ0BRReXpdx_rP3YK8qejgA%3D%3D&expnd=1&sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwjryZD2hcSJAxWuJTQIHbkKEwAQyecJegQIQRAO) or harm.” We could hold this person in “judgement”, remember this “judgement” is an “evil thought” in verse 4. We’re rendering a judgement based on someone’s superficialities. Instead, we overlook whatever that is to love this person.

Think of all the people you interact with on a day. They have different skin color, different languages, different styles of clothing, different jobs, different intelligence levels, different levels of hygiene. Every single person is worthy of what is the “royal law” (v. 8). They are all worthy of love. Love that neighbor just as you would love yourself.

To give an example, maybe I’m working back in retail at Sport Chalet and interacting with a rough guy. The superficialities are telling me to render negative judgement. His breath is bad, he has a bad attitude, he just doesn’t seem to understand what I’m saying because he’s so dull. (Look, even in this statement I’ve rendered negative judgement of this hypothetical person, that’s how deep it goes!) Instead I show him a mercy. I show him love. I patiently work with him to rent him his skiis. Why do I do this?

I render a mercy to this man because mercy will be shown to me. I’m just as bad as this guy, if not worse! Look at the disgusting sin and darkness in my heart, far worse than bad breath.

The reward here? That mercy triumphs over judgement! God’s own mercy will triumph over the judgement rendered. What a good gift. We who are worthy of death and eternal suffering are saved from the flames of Hell, simply because God has chosen to show us mercy.
