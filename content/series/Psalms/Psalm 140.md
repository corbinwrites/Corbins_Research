# Lesson

## Thoughts on Imprecatory Psalms

A question for you to not to answer out loud but to ponder: A *re some Psalms sinful?*We may first balk at a question like this when we consider serene Psalms such as [[Bible/ESV/Psalms/Ps 23|Psalm 23]] (The Lord is my Shepherd) or [[Bible/ESV/Psalms/Ps 51|Psalm 51]] (Create in Me a Clean Heart), but when we encounter imprecatory psalms the question can sound like it has some merit. **Are imprecatory psalms sinful?** Here in this passage David clearly calls judgement on the wicked. Aren’t we supposed to pray for them instead? Perhaps some Psalms operate in a similar way to how Judges depicts the sinful acts of Israel.

In the Lord’s providence Elliot Kang has been asked this question twice this year and I had the pleasure of talking through his answer and researching it on my own prior to tonight. Elliot and I share the same answer to this question: **No none of the Psalms are sinful, including this one. Let me give you a few reasons why.**

### The Broad Intent of the Psalms

Let’s not forget that we are reading a songbook intended for the people of God to sing *to *God. It would be quite strange if the Lord allowed for sinful hearts and minds to construct songs of sin that would be sung in worship to him. It would be counter intuitive to the very nature of worship!

### The New Testament Usage of the Psalms

As I’ve mentioned previously with this passage in particular, Paul quotes verse 3 in [[Bible/ESV/Romans/Rom 3#13|Romans 3:13]]. Dr. Robert Godfrey points out that the Psalms are quoted often in the New Testament showing that Jesus and his apostles, find great relevance in the Psalms for New Covenant believers. That goes to say that imprecatory psalms are not withstanding. Jesus even quotes from imprecatory Psalms in two places in John (John 2:17 (Ps 69) and 15:25 (Ps 35:19; 69:4)).

### The Subject of the Imprecatory Psalms

Critical for us to remember as well is that the enemies mentioned in the Psalm is **God’s** enemies. We are not cursing***our own***enemies.

> [!question] ❓
> Is there a difference between an enemy of God and a personal enemy?
> \*There can be! A Venn Diagram of sorts. Enemies of God may not afflict us personally but can still be prayed against.

Alternatively, our own personal enemy, unlike in the Psalms, could be on the side of good and we are on the side of “bad.” For instance a friend confronting us in sin.

Where the Venn Diagram intersects is an interesting one. One where the person is opposed to God and his people AND is afflicting us. \*

### The Purpose of the Imprecatory Psalms

Yes, we can pray against the enemies of God, however it’s not everyday all the time. I’ll let Dr. Robert Godfrey say it in his own words.

> Should we be cursing enemies?
> And I think part of the answer is, well, not all the time. Not a lot of the time. We need to kind of follow the balance of the Psalter. The Psalter doesn’t curse enemies all the time either. Nonetheless, I think we mustn’t sentimentalize Christian ethics. Paul says in Romans, after all, that we love our enemies so that in the final judgment, more coals will be heaped on their heads. So, the loving of the enemy is not the elimination of judgment to come. Loving the enemy is not eliminating the reality that justice has to be maintained in the world.

Godfrey goes on to point out when we pray and sing things like “Come, Lord Jesus” we are explicitly asking for him to come repair our world, but also implicitly, asking for him to judge the wicked. He concludes his thoughts with the following:

> So, as difficult as this is, and I don’t want to minimize the difficulty, it is a prayer for thorough judgment on the wicked, so that the righteous will be protected. And when we think about the final judgment, we realize that that is what’s going to happen in the final judgment. And we have to be sure we don’t become so sentimental that the final judgment itself becomes a problem for us. And in that sense, the Psalms help us even in their imprecations.

**It is a prayer for thorough judgement on the wicked, so that the righteous will be protected**. This is not a Psalm we will pray often (similar to [[Bible/ESV/Psalms/Ps 88|Psalm 88]]), but it is helpful to know that it exists.

## Deliver Me from Evil Men(1-5)

The first request that David delivers to God.

> [!question] ❓
> What do we learn about the evil/violent men in verses 1-5?
> _Their personality, their actions, descriptors, qualities, motivation_

Their intent: We learn that the men persecuting David are not only evil, but violent. They want to do harm to him and the people of God.

Their action: Notice in verse two that their evil is in both in their hearts and their actions. They plan evil and then stir up division and war.

Their language: they have a snake mouth full of venom

Their qualities: They are crafty and arrogant. They seek to hide a trap, but their arrogance is in full display because David knows about it.

> [!question] ❓
> What do we learn about David in verses 1-5?
> _His personality, actions, descriptors, qualities, motivation_

David needs help. He could be worried or weakened. He’s perceptive and sees the actions of his enemies, but carries it to the Lord in prayer. Sitting on the image of the “known trap” it’s quite interesting to me that David sees the trap and knows it well enough to describe it in detail (there are cords tied in the form of a net) but still recognizes that for there to be any success he must carry it to the Lord in prayer.

Speaking for myself here, I would be tempted to “just handle this one for God” if I saw so blatant a trap. I admire this quality in David.

David takes a breath. _Selah_. The congregation singing takes a breath. *Selah.*Both of have sung of the wickedness that they see. Next both David and the congregation singing, move to ask for God’s help.

## Grant Not the Desires of the Wicked (v. 6-8)

David, and those singing this Psalm come out of this pause with a bold declaration of praise. God is *our *God. God is the God of David. God is *our *God. There is a personal connection here.

David invokes this personal connection to ask that the Lord hear his plea! Almost as to say, “God you know me and my struggle, please hear me!” This is the start of his prayer that extends down to verse 11.

This plea does intermingled with praise. David reminds himself, and the Lord, of times when God was previously faithful to David. David calls God “the strength of my salvation.”

> [!question] ❓
> _What does “the strength of my salvation” mean?_
> Only the Lord grants us salvation. It is his effort, in the Messiah to come, that ultimately makes way for our salvation.

David is first and foremost, thankful for his salvation. God has shown himself faithful in offering salvation.

David is second, thankful for how god has “covered my head in the day of battle.”

> [!question] ❓
> *What does this image mean? “Covered my head in the day of battle.”
> *God is a helmet

One wonders if Paul had thought about this when writing about “the helmet of salvation” in [[Bible/ESV/Ephesians/Eph 6#17|Ephesians 6:17]]. In any case, the Lord has protected David in either a literal day of war or perhaps in the metaphorical war against the wicked.

Remembering these times of faithfulness, David carries his second request to God!

> Grant not, O Lord, the desires of the wicked; do not not further their evil plot or they will be exalted!

We learn that those seeking evil against David, and against God, seek to exalt themselves, glorify themselves! This means they seek to supplant God and establish themselves as rulers. Rulers over themselves, rulers over David, rulers over God!

Truly these are wicked self-centered people.

_Selah_. David takes a breath before carrying his final request…

## Let the Mischief of Their Lips Overwhelm Them! (v. 9-11)

David focuses on a body part of the evil men once more. He returns back to their head, face, and lips. He’s previously mentioned their serpentine features in **verse 3.** His final request is for**justice**.

David asks the Lord that He will return the trouble they cause to return back to them. Which is a quite reasonable prayer, “God let them experience the actions of their consequences.”

> [!question] ❓
> Pause. This can seem unreasonable. We should normally pray for God’s grace! However, what is the trouble they are sowing? What makes them an enemy of God?
> v. 2 - Plan evil, stir up wars
> v. 3 - Have poison in their mouth
> v. 5 - Entrapping the People of God (David)
> v. 8 - Self-exaltation (even over God)

So with their wickedness in mind, David asks for something to cover the wicked… **burning coals**. We could be led to believe there is purification imagery here, but we see it’s more of destruction as we see when David asks they be thrown “into fire” and is further proven by the idea that these men should not** rise again**(v. 10) and** not be established** (v.11).

To finish this third request David once again returns to the idea he started with

> let evil hunt down the violent man speedily!

David once again asks that the same evil the men spread be their own undoing. That the evil they spread turns against them. The snare set in the road springs and captures the wicked man who set it. The serpent will bite its own tail and slowly die from the poison.

## I Am Confident that the Lord Will Protect Me (v. 12-13)

David finishes his prayer in verse 11 and ends in confidence. Remembering how the Lord has been faithful to him he *knows *the Lord will maintain the cause of the afflicted and will execute justice.

David certainly had the book of Genesis at this time and knew there was a time where the snake-crusher would come. (Genesis 2:15)

> [[Bible/ESV/Genesis/Gen 3#14|Genesis 3:14-15]]
> [14] The LORD God said to the serpent,
>
> “Because you have done this,
> cursed are you above all livestock
> and above all beasts of the field;
> on your belly you shall go,
> and dust you shall eat
> all the days of your life.
> [15] I will put enmity between you and the woman,
> and between your offspring and her offspring;
> **he shall bruise your head,
> and you shall bruise his heel.”**

God may or may not execute _immediate_ justice for David and the people of Israel, but David, and the congregation singing this Psalm were confident that, in some way, a final judge would arrive and execute perfect Justice. He would crush Satan, the accuser, the first and greatest snake, but would also wipe away the little snakes underneath him as well.

Whether that judgment was near or far, David’s final words are true for Israel then and as Christians today.

> [[Bible/ESV/Psalms/Ps 140#13|Psalm 140:13]]
> [13] Surely the righteous shall give thanks to your name;
> the upright shall dwell in your presence.

As we learned last week, those who follow the Lord are on the side of righteousness. Those that follow God are upright. They will dwell in the presence of the Lord and not be sent into miry pits.

# Application

7. Throughout the passage, David asks for God to protect him (v. 1, 4) despite the obvious traps they set. Is your first inclination to ask God for help or is it your last ditch effort? Why?
8. As a modern day Christian, do you think there is a time to pray a Psalm like this? What would be a situation for this? How does this balance with passages like [[Bible/ESV/Matthew/Matt 5#44|Matt 5:44]] and [[Bible/ESV/Luke/Luke 6#27|Luke 6:27]] (But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you.)
9. Do you feel the same confidence about the the Lord’s deliverance as David has in verses 12 and 13? If not, how can you grow it? If yes, what moments of God’s faithfulness give you this confidence?
