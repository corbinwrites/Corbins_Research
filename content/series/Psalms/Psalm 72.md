## Preamble on Jesus

> [!question] ❓
> Who is this Psalm about?
> King, Jesus, Somewhere in between!

Given that this either towards the end of David’s life or somewhere in Solomon’s life, we can confidently conclude that David, and his people, know of the covenant that God makes with David in **2 Samuel 7:12-15.**

> 2 Samuel 7:12
> 12 When your days are fulfilled and you lie down with your fathers, I will raise up your offspring after you, who shall come from your body, and I will establish his kingdom. 13 He shall build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. 14 I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son. When he commits iniquity, I will discipline him with the rod of men, with the stripes of the sons of men, 15 but my steadfast love will not depart from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I put away from before you.

With this covenant in mind, the Psalmist rather _boldly_ praises for some hefty blessings. Things like “all kings fall down before him” and “all nations call him blessed.” The psalmist and the Hebrew believe, know this king is coming sometime after David. From their perspective, who knows which one it could be! The Hebrew believer knows from Moses that the Messiah will bring land, people, and blessing _and now_ they know the Messiah will be a king with a house established forever, who calls God his father.

So what makes this Psalm interesting is that the Psalmist prays this, the Hebrew sings this, for a hope and understanding that the next king is this. They could sing this for every king that comes down the line, but they will fall short. So, in a distance sense, this prayer is only truly realized at the birth of Jesus. Who is the king that realizes each of these prayers at his final coronation, when he is seated as king over the remade world.

So I give this preamble to say, this is not prophecy, but rather a prayer with near and far implications. F**or the Hebrew believer, they do pray this for the incoming king, whoever he may be, with the hope of him being the fulfillment of 2 Samuel 7. But in a far sense, their prayers \*\*\***are **\***answered in Jesus. \*\*

While teaching tonight, I’ll refer to the main subject in the Psalm as, the King. It is completely fair for you to read me using that title for “King Jesus.” I’ll use that title simply because of the intriguing dual nature of this Psalm.

## A Just Reign (1-4)

The Psalmist considers *Justice *in it’s various dimensions when praying this.

v. 1 The Psalmist begins with a two generation prayer! May the king immediately receive the Lord’s justice (LSB “your judgements.”) May the King receive the judgements on men and women passed down from the Lord. Similarly, my the prince be upright/righteous! He will soon be the king! May he be an upstanding man. This righteousness is not only reserved for the King though, it is also prayed for the king in the next verse.

**Justice used for court room judgement**

v. 2 - Let his judge the people with righteousness. With uprightness. This is the image of someone using a correct, fair scale with officially sanctioned weights. A shekel is a true shekel. It’s difference the scale in the fruit section of the grocery store v. the cash register scale. The one in the fruit section is close, but not precise, but the cash register is precise, to the 10th of a gram. The Psalmist prays that the king treat everything like the cash register scale, not like the fruit section scale.

Similarly he says the poor, those that are week or financially poor are treated with justice _mishpat_. Which we briefly talked about last week. The idea of giving “someone their rights!” that includes foreigners gleaning from the fields and also for those who have had an animal stolen to seek reparation. It’s to the totality of the Law.

**Justice used for providence (social justice)**

> [!question] ❓
> What does it mean for the mountains to bear prosperity?
> *Food
> *Do you see a stipulation for this food to appear?
> _In righteousness_

The king should be righteous and exemplify this quality. Because of *his *righteousness, the people will exemplify righteousness and reap covenant blessing.

Said more simply, let the mountains bear prosperity to the people _when they are in a season of righteousness_. Much like the orchard bears fruit in the fall, so should the hills be white with harvest _when the people are righteous._

**Justice used for defense and punishment**

Finally, the Psalmist prays that the King will *defend *the cause of the poor and give deliverance to the needy.

> [!question] ❓
> What does defend mean?
> _Protect, shield_
> What does deliverance mean?
> _a rescue from bondage or danger_

This king is not only defensive but offensive too as he strikes against the oppressor. The word here is crush, which gives depicts the king as larger in size and stature over the oppressor. Thing of things we crush. We crush bugs, cans, and paper. All smaller things. This King is, metaphorically, far greater than the oppressor.

## A Universal Reign (5-11)

It’s only fitting that such a great king have a reign that befits him. Next we can see how this king’s rule is universal.

This universal reign starts with a small specific group:

> [!question] ❓
> Who is the “they” in verse 5?
> *The children of the needy *tied in the Hebrew parallelism to the _poor of the people_

**The people of God **is where this King’s reign starts. The psalmist prays that they will fear God as long as the sun and the moon endure, to make it more clear, the Psalmist interprets that one for you “for all generations!”

> [!question] ❓
> May he be like rain that falls on the mown grass, like showers that water the earth! What meaneth this?
> _This king is refreshing to his people, life-giving_

To make it more clear the psalmist simply states, may the righteous (those who are with God) flourish! Just like freshly watered grass and may that and peace continue forever!

Next the psalmist moves the scope into a **global** reign.

> [!question] ❓
> Looking at verses 8-11 geographically, is there a trend you see in the places mentioned?
> _They get bigger, the Psalmist starts small and gets larger progressively_

- The Dead Sea, which the River Jordan feeds, to the Mediterranean Sea
- “The River” refers to the Euphrates, and that’s on the far side of the middle east near modern day Iraq
- The desert tribes can refer to Saudia Arabia to the south and Egypt to the West
- Tarshish which is in modern day Spain! From one side of the Mediterranean to the other (2 Chronicles 9:21)
- Sheba, which is extremely rich and on the far side of the Arabian peninsula (1 Kings 10:10)
- All nations, we’re going global

## A Compassionate Reign (12-14)

Next we see that this not only **universal, **but compassionate as well.\*\* **We see the king not only help his people, but everyone. Notice that in verses 12-14 we do not see the phrase “of the people.” That means the king has arrived to help **all \*\*the needy, weak, and poor.

> [!question] ❓
> What does the king do for the needy weak and poor in verses 12-14?

- Delivers the needy, the poor, and those who have no helper
- Pity on the weak and needy
- Saves the lives of the needy
- He redeems their life

Certainly this would be a truly incredible earthly king who has such care. To sit high atop his throne and for him to value the blood of the lowest subject in his kingdom a person who is poor and has no helper. And for that person it says the king finds their life, their blood, _precious_.

> What does precious mean?

This king, who has the treasures of Sheba and Tarshish opened to him, finds the life of his lowest subject to be the same or _greater_ than these treasures he is given. To protect this precious commodity, the king seeks to save, to redeem, the life of all who have need. What a good king we serve!

## A Prosperous Reign (15-17)

The Psalmist similarly breaks out in praise at that comment as well!

In the next three verses (15-17) the Psalmist prays the king has a **prosperous **reign and recaps many of things stated previously in the passage

> [!question] ❓
> What images, ideas, and themes are repeated in this section?

- The people will flourish, blossom (v. 16, v. 7)
- Abundance of crops (v. 16, v. 3)
- His reign will be global (v. 15, v.10) - Even Sheba knows him
- His reign will be forever (v. 17, v.5-6)

As we get to 17, it’s clear that the subject of this prayer is in fact a King, but that this King is the Messiah. The implicit prayer in 17 is that the Messiah will come and be this prayer, because they want the Messiah to endure forever, for people to be blessed in him and for all nations to call him blessed.

## A Glorious Reign (18-20)

There is a division in commentators. This where Collins would say this is a doxological closer for all of Book Two. It’s similar to the Psalm, but doesn’t fit fully, thus would be seen a general statement of praise.

John MacArthur would say this is completely linked to the Psalm and includes is it as the final praise to God for his reign. Admittedly, I’m picking this point of view because I’m enamored with the idea of it. Here, the psalmist rips off the mask he’s been concealing the King behind to show the full glory of the Lord. He blesses the Lord for he has excitement with the King who will fully live out these qualities. All the wondrous things in this Psalm can *only *come about from the Lord, including the provision of this wonderful King.

v. 19 - “his” shifts the subject from King to God… or does it? No commentator is saying this, but it feels almost as a wink from the Psalmist. The Psalmist uses similar language with the name of the King and the name of God going on forever. The Psalmist would know that the Davidic covenant says that God will call the Messiah his Son. Perhaps a wink that the “his” we’ve been reading about deserves the same glory as God, for he is God.

In either case, the psalm crescendos with thanksgiving! May the Earth be filled with his glory. “His glory” is God’s full power revealed, may the earth be filled with it. Amen, “so be it” amen, “so be it.”

The Psalm ends with a footnote. Some say this is because this is the last Psalm David had written up until that point. Others would say it’s just because he’s doing this to close the book. I, and Charles Spurgeon, think this is David’s final prayer. How beautiful that in his final words he chooses to praise the Lord for the work he will do. May we seek to finish our lives in a similar way, singing praise to the Lord for the work he will do far beyond our lifetime and into the generations to come.
