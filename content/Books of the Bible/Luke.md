# How to Study Luke
The Gospel According to Luke is a synoptic Gospel. The word synoptic comes from the Greek *synopsis* which means "seeing together." When reading Matthew, Mark, and Luke, it is evident that they are very similar in form, though differences do appear. This is in stark contrast to John's account, which was most likely written while on Patmos and thus did not confer with the other Gospel writers, which accounts for a stark difference in his account.

## Critical Analysis of the Synoptic Gospels
When we look more closely at the Synoptic Gospels many similarities and some small differences in exact wording, chronology, and content will appear. Why?

Contemporary scholars employee three areas of critical analysis though at different points in history only one may have been employed: Oral, Source, and Redaction.

**Oral Criticism** simply is the observation that by moving the Gospel from an oral tradition to a written tradition some details would get lost and others affirmed. This is partially true, but does not account for the exact phrase matches in each of the Synoptic Gospels.
**Conclusion:** Helpful! But don't stop here!

**Source Criticism** is the observation that Matthew, Mark, and Luke would have gathered from similar *and* different sources accounting for differences. There are many explanations for how each writer sourced information for writing the Gospel. The prevailing theory is *Interdependence*. Each Gospel writer used the work of another Gospel writer(s) to construct his own Gospel. There are many theories as to the correct order and you can research them on your own if you're interested, but two stand above the rest as the most defensible, *The Augustinian Proposal* and *The "Two-Gospel Hypothesis"*. D.A. Carson and Douglas Moo would commend *the Two-Gospel Hypothesis* as the foremost theory which would place Mark as the first book written with Matthew and Luke later following. Luke, in addition to using Mark's account, would have also sourced from a book of written "Jesus Sayings" that was floating around back as well as his own "special sauce" of sources he writes to Theophilus about. Carson & Moo clearly outline that this is a "working theory" and certainly not concrete.
**Conclusion:** Helpful! But don't stop here either!

![[Pasted image 20260910131011.png|243]]

**Redaction Criticism** is the observation that Matthew, Mark, and Luke are distinct authors writing to a distinct audience. As an author each man, being led by the Holy Spirit, would tailor the Gospel in a way that taught that people best. This is by far the most popular critical lens in the modern era. We should carefully handle this critical lens and not over correct our vision as to receive a headache or to not apply it at all and continue to not see at all.
**Overcorrection:** We read *too* deeply into an omission or addition from Luke and to claim there is theological motivation. It *could* be the case, but it we should be careful to declare that so.

> [!example] An Example of Redaction Overcorrection
> In Matthew 8 there are two men which are in Gerasenes when Jesus send Legion into the pigs. In Luke 8, the author only mentions one. Clearly this is because Matthew is trying to show that within us are two spirits, two wolves if you will, and Jesus is able to overcome them both. The spirit and the flesh. 
> **No!** Matthew mentions _both_ men who were present, while Mark and Luke focus exclusively on the single, more vocal individual who spoke with Jesus and later went on to preach throughout the Decapolis.

**Undercorrection:** We claim that the author has *not* tailored the message and thus the omissions and additions make for an "inferior" Gospel that we cannot trust. We *can* trust that he author has tailored this message for us and that is helpful and good. He has done so in a way that has handled the historicity well and still truly represents Jesus.
**Conclusion:** Helpful! Use the previous two criticisms and don't overly lean on this!

> [!example] An Example of Redaction Undercorrection
> Luke omits Jesus walking on the water on the Sea of Galilee. So this is an inferior Gospel, it's inaccurate and should be thrown out.
> **No**, Mark existed first. Why rehash what Mark already wrote?


**Final thoughts on the blend of Gospel Critique:**
The modern bible scholar should apply all *three* methods to extract the most from the Gospel. These three methods show us:
- Oral tradition moving into written word
- Sources like Mark and Q have allowed Luke to give us *more* insight into the life of Jesus 
- Redactions have provided Luke *more* space to tell us things we didn't know about Jesus and also to give us the Gospel from a Gentile point of view.

It is a beautiful fact that the account of Jesus is not in one Gospel account but in **four**. "The story of Jesus has come to us not in one super-gospel but in four gospels each with its own distinct and important contribution to make our understand of Jesus... this fourfold gospel should be appreciated for the richness of perspective it brings. 'Jesus is such a gigantic figure that we need all four portraits to discern him.'" (P. 112, Carson & Moo)

With wisdom from above, God wrote these individual accounts in such a special way that accurately show the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus in a historically accurate way.

## Genre of the Gospel
Most closely aligns to ancient *biographies*. Though modern biographies today are very focused on how the person came to be, these quickly skip past Jesus' childhood, something that was common in ancient biographies. Then writers focused on the highlights of the person. That being said, the gospels are unique in that the are "anonymous" (in that an ancient biographer would frequently made big deal about himself) and that there is the combination of teaching and action.

While the Gospel accounts have the hallmarks of a narrative and it can be tempting to view each story in the classic narrative structure, it's important to keep in mind that is modern convention being applied to a text that predates it. Careful exegesis and an understanding of the history will serve us helpful companions while using literary analysis to study a gospel!

# Luke
The longest book in the New Testament and we follow the same four narrative arcs seen in Mark: 
- Jesus’ preparation for ministry
- His ministry in Galilee
- His movement to Jerusalem
- His death
- His resurrection

Luke provides the most detail of the synoptic Gospels about Jesus’ trip to Jerusalem. Luke also includes three parables not found in the others: The Good Samaritan, The Prodigal Son, and the Shrewd Manager. Also noteworthy is that Luke is a companion to the later book of Acts. The two work in concert to show how the Gospel comes first to the Jews and then the whole (known) world!

Luke is most likely a Gentile. We learn this from Colossians 4:10-14, where Paul does not account Epaphras and Luke as “of the circumcision.” Additionally, in Luke 1, it can be surmised that Luke, the Gentile is writing to Gentiles. Luke’s Gospel opens with a letter to Theophilius, Greek for “Lover of God.” This, probable, Greek patron stands as a representation for the audience, Gentiles. If this is true, what a joy to read from a Gentile, written for Gentiles, as Gentiles. This book was most likely written in the 60s AD just before the fall of Jerusalem and Nero’s Persecution of Christians (both, notably, lacking from Luke’s account).

Only Luke's account tells the entire Jesus story from John the Baptist’s birth, to Jesus’ ascension. Luke also prominently features the stories of women and how they are major players in the gospel story!
## Distinct Themes to Luke’s Gospel
- The Great Reversal - *The lowly exalted, the proud humbled*
- The Better Adam - *Jesus does what Adam and Israel could not do*
- The Centrality of God’s Plan in the Gospel - *look for the phrase “it is necessary”*
- The Present Reality of Salvation - *Jesus is here now and knocking*
- Gentiles are recipients of God’s Salvation - *Take a look at how the lineage goes beyond Moses and the types of people Jesus helps*
